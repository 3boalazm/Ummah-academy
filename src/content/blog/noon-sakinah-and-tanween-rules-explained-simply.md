Noon Sakinah (a still, unvowelled Noon: ن) and Tanween (the double-vowel endings: ً ٍ ٌ) are two of the very first Tajweed topics beginners encounter, mostly because they appear constantly in nearly every page of the Quran. Here’s a plain-language walkthrough of the four rules that govern them.

This is one piece of a bigger picture — see [Tajweed Rules for Beginners](/tajweed-rules-for-beginners-a-simple-introduction-to-reciting-the-quran-correctly/) for how it fits alongside Makharij and Madd, and [Why Tajweed Matters](/why-tajweed-matters-5-real-benefits-of-reciting-the-quran-correctly/) for why getting it right matters beyond correctness for its own sake.

## Why These Two Rules Are Grouped Together

Noon Sakinah and Tanween are treated identically in Tajweed because they produce the same sound — a plain “n” — and follow the exact same four rules depending on which letter comes right after them. Once you learn the rule for Noon Sakinah, you already know the rule for Tanween in the same situation.

## 1. Izhar (Clear Pronunciation) — إظهار

When the Noon Sakinah or Tanween is followed by one of the throat letters (ء ه ع ح غ خ), it’s pronounced clearly and fully, with no change or blending — this is the “default,” most straightforward case.

## 2. Idgham (Merging) — إدغام

When followed by one of six specific letters (ي ر م ل و ن), the Noon sound merges into the next letter rather than being pronounced separately. Idgham itself splits into two types — with and without a nasal sound (Ghunnah) — depending on which of the six letters follows.

## 3. Iqlab (Conversion) — إقلاب

When followed specifically by the letter Ba (ب), the Noon sound converts into a Meem sound, pronounced with a light nasal quality. This is the rarest of the four rules but very distinctive once you learn to recognize it — you’ll often see a small meem symbol written above the Noon in a properly marked Mushaf as a visual cue.

## 4. Ikhfa (Concealment) — إخفاء

For the remaining 15 letters not covered by the three rules above, the Noon is neither fully pronounced (as in Izhar) nor merged away (as in Idgham) — it’s partially concealed, pronounced with a light nasal sound that sits between the two. This is usually the hardest of the four rules for beginners to get consistently right, since it requires a sound that isn’t quite either extreme.

## A Simple Way to Practice

Rather than memorizing all four rules as an abstract list, most students find it easier to:

- Pick one short surah you already know well.
- Go through it slowly, identifying every Noon Sakinah and Tanween.
- Say out loud which rule applies and why, before reciting that word.

Doing this with real verses — rather than isolated flashcards — is what actually makes the rules stick.
